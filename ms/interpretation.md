

# The paper in its own words

Every table in Dugar, Bhattacharya and Reiley (2012) reproduces from the files they supplied. The sentences
written on top of the tables do not survive the tables. This document sets the paper's own words, quoted
from the December 2010 final draft (and once from the May 2008 draft), beside the numbers in
[`output/`](../output). Section and footnote references follow the 2010 draft. The full audit is in
[review.md](review.md).

## 1. The mechanism is asserted, and the data contradict it

> The genesis of this discrimination can be attributed to a high-status female's concern for a decline in her social status if she engages in a marital relationship with a low-status male.

(§1.) If concern for status loss drives caste sorting, families below the top should welcome grooms above
them. They do not. Per advertisement, Kayastha families send 14.1% of their letters to the
Kayastha groom and 8.9% to the Brahmin; Namasudra families send 19.6% to their own,
10.0% to the Kayastha and 3.8% to the Brahmin. On the paper's own sorting scale,
Namasudra families sort as hard as Brahmin families (index 0.38 against 0.38), and they can
only be avoiding grooms *above* them. A mechanism that runs on fear of falling cannot produce a refusal to
climb. The behaviour on all three sides is the same behaviour: families write to their own caste.

## 2. The definition of discrimination decides the finding

> Status-based discrimination is said to arise in our context if responders from a given high-caste group send significantly lower number of responses to a lower-caste groom relative to their own-caste groom despite the fact that the own-caste groom is otherwise observationally identical to that lower-caste groom.

(§1.) And in §4.1:

> Since LCR are at the bottom of the caste ladder, they do not have the
> opportunity to discriminate as per the above definition.

Only downward avoidance counts. Upward avoidance, which is at least as strong, is defined out of existence
before any table is shown, and the lowest caste is declared unable to discriminate by construction. Apply a
symmetric definition, own-caste share against the caste-blind third, and all three castes discriminate:
Brahmin 0.38, Kayastha 0.13, Namasudra 0.38. The paper's "status-based discrimination
by higher-caste females" is one third of a symmetric pattern, labelled as though it were the whole.

## 3. The most informative fact in the data is a footnote

> a specific lower caste group sends the smallest proportion of their responses to the highest caste grooms in our design, which appears counterintuitive, as there exists a strong incentive for a lower caste female to marry a higher caste groom to improve her caste status.

(Footnote 13.) This is not a curiosity. Namasudra families' letters to the Brahmin groom fall from
7.7% of their letters at Rs 7,000 to 1.1% at
Rs 35,000, an income elasticity of -1.21 (95% interval
-2.01 to -0.56). Kayastha families
at Rs 35,000 send the Namasudra groom 15.5% and the Brahmin groom 7.2%. A
rich higher-caste groom is the least attractive rich groom to the families below him. The result is
counterintuitive only from inside the paper's premise; it is the plainest fact in Table 4, and it gets
four lines at the bottom of a page.

One might read this as a bias against Brahmins. The income gradient says otherwise. Kayastha families face
one caste step in each direction. At Rs 7,000 they send the Brahmin groom 10.4%
of their letters and the Namasudra groom 7.2%: a poor Brahmin is the more
attractive of the two. At Rs 35,000 the order flips, 7.2% against
15.5%. A taste against Brahmins would not depend on the Brahmin's income. What
depends on his income is whether a family expects him to answer, and what he would cost. Letters to grooms
above the writer fall as the groom gets richer; letters to grooms below rise. Brahmin families' three curves
all rise only because there is no groom above a Brahmin in the design.

## 4. The paper's own escape hatch sits under its headline too

> The observed response pattern of the lower caste responders to higher caste grooms can be a combination of the status incentive and a discouragement effect that may arise due to lower expected call-back rate from higher caste grooms. The empirical identification of these effects would need data on men's preferences, which we do not have.

(Footnote 13, continued.) Correct, and it applies everywhere. A letter is written by a family that wants a
match *and* expects a reply. The paper invokes expected call-back only where the data embarrass the model.
The same term sits under every downward letter: a Brahmin family writing to a rich Namasudra groom may be
warming to money, or guessing that a rich Namasudra family is the one that might accept a Brahmin bride.
Nothing in the design separates the two in either direction. The sentence that admits this for lower-caste
writers is the sentence that undoes the headline for higher-caste writers.

## 5. "Substitutability" is a label attached to a slope

> This finding extends major support to our key hypothesis that there exists substitutability between loss in caste-status and gain in terms of the groom-income on the part of higher-status females.

(§1.) The slope is real. Brahmin families' share of letters to the Namasudra groom rises from
1.0% to 10.7%, an elasticity of
1.43, against 0.16 for their own
groom. What the slope shows is that advertised income moves first letters across caste lines downward.
Calling that a substitution of status for income requires the letter to be a preference, which item 4
concedes it need not be, and requires the quantity given up to be status, which items 1 and 3 show is not
what families are trading. Money buys letters from the small minority of Brahmin families willing to write
down; whether it buys anything from the majority is not observed.

## 6. The choice set was not nine advertisements

> how much additional monthly income a groom with a caste status c2 and a monthly income of y would need in order to achieve the same probability of obtaining a response from responders with caste status c1, as that of a groom with a caste status c1 and a monthly income of y?

(§4.3, the question Table 6 answers.) And from the note to Table 6:

> Figure in each shaded cell denotes on average how much additional monthly income (in Rs. thousand) a given lower-caste groom, belonging to a specific income category, would need in order to compensate a given higher-caste responder group so that that responder responds to him with the same probability as to a groom from her own caste belonging to the same income category.

"Probability of obtaining a response" is a count of families, and "compensate" turns that count into a
trade-off inside one family's head. Nothing of the kind was observed. The paper itself describes the
market:

> On an average, 1000 matrimonial advertisements are published in every Sunday edition of this newspaper.

(§2.) About 370 of those were from grooms, sorted into caste columns, and a letter cost Rs 3 to 7.50. Take
the two cells behind the headline. Among the 478 letters from Brahmin families, 110 went to the Brahmin
groom at Rs 35,000 and 51 to the Namasudra groom at Rs 35,000. Those are different households; no family
was shown both grooms and asked to choose. Each of them scanned a column holding dozens of Brahmin grooms at
every income, and at Rs 5 a letter wrote to every ad that cleared its bar. A letter to the fictitious
Namasudra ad records that it cleared the bar for that family; a letter to the fictitious Brahmin ad records
the same for another family. The 110 and the 51 therefore count how many families found each ad acceptable
that week. Equalising the two counts by raising the Namasudra groom's income answers a market question, how
rich he must be for as many Brahmin families to consider him, and even that only if the income lies within
the range tried, which it does not. It does not answer what any family would accept in exchange for caste,
because every family in the data could also write, that same week, to a rich Brahmin, and most of the
families who wrote to a poor Brahmin gave up nothing by doing so. When the paper argues in §5 that

> a higher-caste female who responded to the HI groom of a lower-caste group also had the option of responding to the HI groom from her own caste as all the nine advertisements appeared in the same edition of the newspaper

it treats the nine as her menu. She had the option of both, and of several hundred more. Banerjee, Duflo,
Ghatak and Lafortune can quote an income premium because they watch one advertiser rank the letters it
actually received: a choice, within one family, over a real set. What these counts support is how many
families of each caste found each ad worth a letter, and how that number moves with income.

## 7. The price has no interval, no support, and no model

> However, the magnitude of increase in these response probabilities are quite small, as we find that per thousand Rupees increase in income of a
> lower-caste groom increases the probability of a higher-caste response between 0.2% to 0.5%. These low probability figures imply that the amounts of additional monthly income that lower-caste grooms need to offer to higher-caste responders to completely offset the discriminatory response patterns are enormous and according to our estimates they lie in the range of approximately Rs. 17000 to Rs. 49000.

(§1.) And in §4.3:

> This means that the LCG-HI, has to almost double his monthly income in order to receive preliminary considerations by HCR at par with the HCG-HI. These massive trade-off magnitudes suggest a strong presence of caste-based discrimination in this Indian marriage market, which is very challenging to weaken with economic incentives.

The "0.2% to 0.5%" are share points per thousand rupees from lines through three income levels; the
"probability" is the share of a caste's letters. Each Table 6 figure divides a share gap by one such slope.
The headline cell, Rs 35,900, has a 95% bootstrap interval of Rs 17,300 to
67,900. Closing the same gap with the medium-to-high slope gives Rs 45,400,
with the low-to-medium slope Rs 23,600, and with a logit that fits the nine cells well
Rs 12,500. The implied income, Rs 70,900, exceeds every one of
the 1,261 real advertisements the authors collected; the highest was Rs 50,000. "Almost double" is one point
in a range that spans a factor of four, computed at an income no real groom advertised, with no uncertainty
reported anywhere in the paper.

## 8. Two ordering claims the data cannot rank

> First, the estimated amount of compensation needed by a HCR to consider the marital advertisement by a lower-caste groom decreases monotonically with an increase in the monthly income of a lower-caste groom.

> This implies that the higher is the status-gap between the responder and the prospective groom, the higher would be the compensation required from the potential groom.

(§4.3.) Both are differences between ratios of small shares. The Namasudra-minus-Kayastha compensation at
Rs 35,000 is 8.5 thousand with a bootstrap interval of
-26 to 43; the fall from
Rs 7,000 to Rs 35,000 for the Namasudra groom is 13.4 thousand with an
interval of -6 to 30. Both
include zero. "Monotonically" describes three point estimates; the data do not order them.

## 9. The supply-side rebuttal rests on letters the paper threw away

The §5 sentence quoted in item 6, that a higher-caste family "also had the option of responding to the HI
groom from her own caste", is offered to rule out a shortage of rich own-caste grooms. The record shows each
family taking exactly one of the nine. The families that used the option the argument relies on, writing to
more than one of the ads, are the ones the analysis excludes: the file drops
243
letters under a `repeat` flag, against the text's 47. Those letters go disproportionately to lower-caste,
high-income grooms. With them kept, the headline compensation falls from
Rs 35,900 to Rs 19,800.

## 10. Dowry is dismissed by a table that cannot test it

> If expected dowry is the prime driver of the responses, then we would expect to observe fewer responses from a given high-caste group for lower-caste grooms with higher income. An inspection of Table 6 contradicts such a view.

(§5.) Groom price rises with the groom's caste and income. That predicts fewer letters from families who
cannot pay as the groom gets richer, which is what the *upward* cells show (item 3). Table 6 contains only
downward cells, where a Brahmin family, on average richer, faces a Namasudra groom who commands less. The
table cited cannot bear on the objection, and the cells that can support it.

## 11. Letters are read as marriages

> still it is natural to assume a positive correlation between the frequency of initial responses and the likelihood of final matches, after allowing for some level of friction in the mate selection process.

(§5.) The correlation may well be positive. It does not make a count of Rs 5 letters into a measure of
what any family would accept in a marriage. Banerjee et al. find, from the advertisers who received such
letters, that families choose among enough own-caste candidates that keeping caste costs them little in
partner income. A family that writes to a poor own-caste groom this week has given up nothing; the rich
own-caste groom will be in next week's edition. The paper's Table 6, and any inversion of it, reads a head
count as a sacrifice. Neither is.

## 12. The same table, opposite policy conclusions

From the May 2008 abstract:

> These results indicate that affirmative action policies in India, which seek to enhance the income of the lower-caste population, are not likely to produce any significant change in inter-caste marriage.

From the December 2010 discussion:

> Thus, the second result suggests that if the phenomenal economic growth in India succeeds in reducing inter-caste economic
> disparity it may help in reducing caste-based discrimination in this marriage market.

Table 6 is the same in both drafts. The first reads Rs 49,000 on a Rs 7,000 base as eightfold and hopeless;
the second reads Rs 36,000 on a Rs 35,000 base as double and promising. Neither sentence is carried by the
data, which say only that among first letters from Brahmin families, a Namasudra groom on Rs 35,000 draws
10.7% against the Brahmin groom's 23.0%, and that
47% of all first letters already cross caste.

## 13. "First", with the neighbour in a footnote

> In this sense, our study is the first one that estimates the trade-off between status and income.

(Footnote 2.) On Banerjee, Duflo, Ghatak and Lafortune, who studied letters in the same newspaper five years
earlier:

> However, they do not explicitly investigate any potential trade-off that may exist between income and caste-status like we do by systematically controlling for grooms' income and caste features.

(Footnote 4.) Banerjee et al. estimate an outside-caste income premium from advertisers' decisions about
which letters to pursue, with an interval, and conclude that the preference is for one's own caste rather
than for higher castes. That is the reading these letters support too. The two papers observe the two sides
of one market; read together they say the same thing, and it is not what this paper's abstract says.

## What the abstract should say

Nine fictitious grooms, three castes by three incomes, drew 1,123 first letters from brides' families in a
Bengali newspaper. Families of every caste wrote mostly to their own: an own-caste advertisement drew 1.4 to
5 times the letters of any other, and no caste wrote up. Advertised income moved letters across caste lines
only downward, with an elasticity of about 1.4 for Brahmin families
writing to a Namasudra groom, and pushed lower-caste letters away from richer higher-caste grooms. Forty-seven
percent of first letters crossed caste. The nine ads sat among hundreds, so counts of letters across families
price no trade-off any family made, and letters do not separate what a family wants from what it expects
to get.
